# Audit finds no transferable fire-stream efficiency

**No primary source audited establishes a measured, transferable fraction of applied fireground water that evaporates or absorbs heat.** The number most often cited as “Särdqvist 0.2” is a planning assumption in Särdqvist’s 1996 Lund report, applied to a **10.8 MJ/kg flame-extinction capacity** (3.6 MJ/kg × 3), not a 2002-book value and not a 20% evaporation or contact fraction ([Särdqvist 1996, pp. 55–56](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=61)). The only controlled dataset found that yields an extinction ratio is four small-room NIST tests, from which Särdqvist derived **0.11, 0.24 and 0.29** against an extinction capacity that already assumes only about 30–35% of the fire’s heat must be removed; he calls **about 0.3 an upper limit**, not a general efficiency ([Särdqvist 1996, pp. 52–53](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=58); [NISTIR 4625](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir4625.pdf)). Other measured and modelled evidence includes stairwell gas cooling with **about 3% or less of the water evaporated**, modelled heat absorption of **40–70%** in a water-mist tunnel, and a heat-balance ratio that includes runoff sensible heat; none measures fireground suppression efficiency. A math-only engine can still compute the **163.21 kW per US gpm** full-vaporization reference and compare powers, but any heat-absorption efficiency must be an explicit scenario assumption or a narrowly matched measurement, **never a default**, and a ratio of 1 is not extinction.

## Four small-room tests anchor a ratio near 0.3

The experiments behind the only controlled efficiency-like ratio are D. W. Stroup and D. D. Evans’s NISTIR 4625, *Suppression of Post-Flashover Compartment Fires Using Manually Applied Water Sprays* (July 1991). The apparatus was a **2.44 m cube burn room** opening to a 12.8 m corridor, with nominally identical wood-crib fires and a hand-held variable-pattern nozzle; the report measured gas and wall temperatures, oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, water flow and drop size ([NISTIR 4625](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir4625.pdf)). It did **not** report an efficiency, and its extracted text contains no occurrence of the word. The ratios are Särdqvist’s later derivation.

Särdqvist writes that “The effectiveness is the relation between the actual and the maximum theoretical value of the heat absorption capacity of the extinguishing media” ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 49](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=55)), but the Table 6 ratios add a further ×3 extinction convention to that denominator. The distinction matters when the numbers are reused.

Särdqvist’s Table 6 values reproduce **extinction HRR ÷ theoretical extinction capacity**, where the theoretical capacity is **3 × water flow × theoretical absorption capacity** ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 53](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=59)). The factor of 3 comes from his statement that removing **30–35% of the released energy** is sufficient for diffusion flames, so the absorption capacity is multiplied by 3 to obtain an extinction capacity ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 52](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=58)). The derived values are:

| Water flow (kg/s) | Theoretical absorption (MJ/kg) | Theoretical extinction capacity (MW) | HRR at extinction (MW) | Derived η |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.320 | 4.08 | 16.0 | 1.8 | **0.11** |
| 0.608 | 4.04 | 7.4 | 1.8 | **0.24** |
| 0.608 | 4.00 | 7.3 | 2.1 | **0.29** |
| 0.272 | 3.88 | 3.2 | 2.2 | 0.69, no control; excluded |

Särdqvist does not print that formula; the reconstruction matches the printed values to two decimals. The **0.69 row is not an efficiency**: the fire was not controlled, so the ratio has no extinction interpretation. The 0.24 and 0.29 rows are the “just able to” cases, and Särdqvist states that the maximum efficiency factor “proved to be about 0.3” while cautioning that the tests were “performed in a small room and under the best of circumstances” and that a larger nozzle in a larger room would face a distribution problem ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 53](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=59)). That is a **stated upper limit from a narrow apparatus**, not a general measured efficiency.

The ratio is also easy to misread. The 0.11 value does not mean 11% of the water’s physical absorption capacity. The implied fraction of the tabulated absorption capacity used at extinction is **η × 3**: about 0.33 for the first test, or roughly **1.35 MJ/kg of the 4.08 MJ/kg** reference. That is why these numbers cannot be dropped into a model that applies η directly to 2.6 or 3.6 MJ/kg without reconciling the ×3 convention and the reference temperature ([Särdqvist 1996, pp. 52–56](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=58)).

## The repeated percentages are assumptions, calibrations, or different quantities

### The 0.2 is a 1996 assumption on a tripled denominator

Särdqvist’s 1996 report gives the exact planning bullets: the maximum heat absorption for water is `2.6 + (T − 100) × 0.00201 MJ/kg`; for droplets smaller than 1 mm, `T` is the smoke temperature, and using 600 °C gives **3.6 MJ/kg**, “which is multiplied with a factor of 3, giving **10.8 MJ/kg in flame extinction capacity**”; an efficiency factor of **0.2** is used for interior fog nozzles; for droplets larger than 2 mm, `T` is 100 °C and the maximum is **2.6 MJ/kg**, with factors of 0.2 for long-range monitors, 0.3 for long-range nozzles and 0.4 for short-range nozzles ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 55](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=61)). Table 7 confirms the arithmetic on p. 56: a 5.0 kg/s fog stream gives `5.0 × 10.8 × 0.2 = 10.8 MW` (printed 11), and a 7.9 kg/s stream gives `7.9 × 10.8 × 0.2 = 17.1 MW` (printed 17) ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 56](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=62)).

The consequence is definitional. For fog, **0.2 × 10.8 = 2.16 MJ/kg**, which is **60% of the 3.6 MJ/kg physical enthalpy** to 600 °C steam, not 20%. For large droplets, the same 0.2 is applied to 2.6 MJ/kg and gives 0.52 MJ/kg. A bare “η = 0.2” is therefore ambiguous by roughly a factor of four depending on the denominator. Särdqvist is explicit that these are estimates: “there are no systematic measurements of the actual extinction capacity of water. Thus, the efficiency must be estimated,” and the factor varies with method, hose operator and equipment ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 55](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=61)). His “100 times more water than should be required theoretically” is a real-fire water-use multiplier, not an efficiency ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 54](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=60)).

### The 2002 book contains no numeric water efficiency

The 2002 English edition of *Water and Other Extinguishing Agents* does not repeat the 0.2/0.3/0.4 table. Its index has “Efficiency, pumps 67–68” but no water-efficiency entry ([book index, p. 332](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=334)). Its water chapters are qualitative: application is described through hot smoke, flames, fuel surface, unaffected fuel and hot surfaces, with no efficiency percentage ([book pp. 147–150](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=149)). The book’s **43 kW/m²** example is a theoretical maximum assuming all water vaporises at 1 L/m²min, immediately followed by “Normally only part of the water vaporises” ([book p. 167](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=169)). Its 70/30 figure is an illustrative gas-volume split, not an efficiency ([book p. 154](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=156)); its 45% and 10–40% figures are **fire-side heat fractions** that must be removed for premixed and diffusion flames, not water fractions ([book p. 314](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=316)).

Most tellingly, the book defines `c_w` as the “proportion of water reaching and vaporising at the fuel surface” and then says “**There is no data available for c_w**” ([book pp. 315–316](https://www.mcf.se/siteassets/dokument/publikationer/english-publications/water-and-other-extinguishing-agents.pdf#page=317)). The 2002 book can support the conceptual distinction between contact, evaporation and cooling; it cannot supply a numeric efficiency.

### 0.32, 0.50, 0.75 and 0.385 are design values, not measurements

The **0.32** comes from Barnett et al., *Fire Safety in Tall Buildings* (CTBUH/McGraw-Hill, 1992), quoted by Särdqvist as a fuel-surface cooling assumption: steam is assumed not to be heated above 100 °C, giving an effective **0.8 MJ/kg**, and `0.32 × 2.6 ≈ 0.83 ≈ 0.84 MW per L/s` ([Särdqvist 1996, p. 54](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/5735831/1048740.pdf#page=60)). The 1992 book itself was not obtained; the value is known only through Särdqvist’s citation.

The **0.50** is a recommendation in Barnett’s SFPE (NZ) Technical Publication **TP 2004/1**, p. 6: “a figure of 50% is recommended for jet nozzle use,” alongside a separate design reserve: 50% for suppression plus 50% extra for exposures, leaks and wind ([TP 2004/1](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/sfpe-TP2004-1.pdf)). The **0.75** is an illustrative fog-nozzle case in the same document and in Grimwood (2005); it is not the value used to derive the 0.385 flow factor ([TP 2004/1, p. 6](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/sfpe-TP2004-1.pdf); [Grimwood 2005, footer p. 26 / PDF p. 30](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/FLOW-RATE%20202004.pdf#page=30)). Grimwood’s 2010 retrospective gives the provenance: the draft used 0.58 from a 0.33 factor, and the final **0.50 was used to produce a flow factor of 0.38** consistent with the TFR 1989 data from 100 serious London fires ([FPA 2010](https://www.thefpa.co.uk/fire-and-risk-management-journal/feature-articles/flow-method)). That is a calibration to observed tactical flows, not a calorimetric measurement, and the TP itself contains no experimental dataset or uncertainty bound for the 50% or 75%.

The **0.385 L/s/MW** is a required-flow factor, not an efficiency: `F = kF / (kW × QW) = 0.50 / (0.50 × 2.6) = 0.3846 L/s per MW` of compartment HRR ([TP 2004/1, p. 12](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/sfpe-TP2004-1.pdf)). Inverting it gives 2.6 MW per L/s, which is the theoretical absorption, not the effective fireground absorption. Example 11.1 in TP 2004/1 and Example 8 in Grimwood also divide by the fire-efficiency factor and label the result “absorbed,” when the water-side absorption is only half that value; the larger number is the controllable fire HRR ([TP 2004/1, p. 10](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/sfpe-TP2004-1.pdf); [Grimwood 2005, footer p. 29 / PDF p. 33](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/FLOW-RATE%20202004.pdf#page=33)).

### The 20% contact figure and the H&R range are not verified measurements

TP 2004/1 p. 6 also says “As little as 20% of the water flow may actually strike the burning fuel surface [8],” citing Särdqvist and Holmstedt, “Water for Manual Fire Suppression,” *Journal of Fire Protection Engineering* 11 (2001) ([TP 2004/1](https://www.highrisefire.co.uk/docs/sfpe-TP2004-1.pdf)). That is a **contact/delivery fraction**, not an evaporation or thermal efficiency, and the primary paper was paywalled and not obtained in this audit; its abstract does not mention 20%. The figure remains unverified at primary level and must not be merged with the 1996 0.2.

The **0.3–0.6** range attributed to Hadjisophocleous and Richardson (2005) is also not a measured range. Their *Fire Technology* paper is closed access, with no open repository copy found; secondary sources disagree, with Canterbury’s 2020 water-supply report describing η as “usually selected in a range of 0.3 to 0.6” for different nozzles and pointing to Särdqvist for guidance, while Hartin (2009) writes 0.2–0.6 by conflating Särdqvist with Barnett/Grimwood ([Canterbury 2020, §2.1.16](https://www.fireandemergency.nz/assets/Documents/Files/Report_175_Assessing_Firefighting_Water_Supplies.pdf); [Hartin 2009](https://commandcompetence.com/post/effective-and-efficient-fire-streams/); [H&R DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10694-005-1269-6)). The primary text could not be inspected, and the accessible record supplies neither the values nor a measurement protocol.

| 0.2 for interior fog | Särdqvist 1996 pp. 55–56, primary | Planning assumption on 10.8 MJ/kg | Measured evaporation or contact fraction |
| 0.32 / 0.84 MW per L/s | Barnett et al. 1992 via Särdqvist 1996 p. 54; book not obtained | Fuel-surface design assumption | Measurement |

| 0.3–0.6 H&R | H&R 2005 closed; Canterbury 2020 and Hartin 2009 secondary | Selected model input, secondary description | Measured range |

**Source status matters here.** Särdqvist 1996, NISTIR 4625, NBSIR 81-2202, Blanchard et al., Wighus (1991) and Wighus and Aune (1995) were inspected as primary texts; TP 2004/1 and Grimwood (2005) were inspected as primary design documents but are not measurements; FPA 2010 is the author’s retrospective; Hartin 2009, Canterbury 2020 and the Hessen/IChemE report are secondary; H&R 2005, Särdqvist and Holmstedt (2001) and Barnett et al. (1992) were inaccessible. The 2002 Särdqvist book is an image-only scan: its relevant water chapters, theory section and index were inspected as page images, not full-book OCR.

## Measured and modelled evidence quantifies cooling, not suppression efficiency

The clearest direct mass-fraction measurement found is much older and scoped to gas cooling. Cooper and O’Neill’s NBSIR 81-2202, *Fire Tests of Stairwell-Sprinkler Systems* (1981), instrumented a three-story stairtower with a propane burner up to about 4 MW, water curtains and spray nozzles, and defined **water usage efficiency as `η_w = G_evap / G`**, the evaporated fraction of the delivered water, derived from an energy balance on the gas stream. The report concludes that “for the purpose of evaporation cooling the components under review have very poor water usage efficiencies … the values η_w are of the order of **.03 or less**,” while cooling efficiency `η_c` could reach about 1.0 ([NBSIR 81-2202, report p. 33](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir81-2202.pdf)). This is **stairwell gas cooling, not fire suppression**, and its zero-humidity inlet assumption and neglect of sensible heating are approximations. It is still important evidence: effective cooling can coexist with only a few percent evaporation.

The closest modern heat-absorption figure is model-derived. Blanchard, Desanghere and Bouley reconstructed a one-third-scale water-mist tunnel test in FDS 5.4.0, with a heptane pool and about 33 L/min of mist, and defined efficiency as heat absorbed by droplets—including gas cooling, radiation attenuation and wall cooling—divided by the heat that would be absorbed if all injected water evaporated. They report **40–70%, time average 53%**, with about 73% of the absorbed energy from the gas, 18% from radiation and 9% from walls; the fire was **not suppressed** ([Blanchard et al. 2011](https://publications.iafss.org/publications/fss/10/119)). This is neither a mass-evaporated fraction nor a hose-stream measurement, and the underlying experiment did not directly measure the water-side energy balance.

Wighus’s SHAR is the best measured heat-balance proxy, but it is not a fraction of water evaporated. SHAR is heat absorbed by the spray divided by fire heat release, and the water-side heat includes heating the evaporated fraction, evaporation, superheating steam **and heating runoff water**. In a 2.5 × 2.5 × 5 m enclosure with a 1 MW propane fire, Wighus (1991) found peak SHAR about 0.5–0.6 and a characteristic above **0.7** if extinguishment was not instantaneous ([Wighus 1991](https://publications.iafss.org/publications/fss/3/997)). Wighus and Aune (1995) report downward-spray extinguishment when SHAR exceeded **0.3**, with 0.15 not extinguishing ([Wighus & Aune 1995](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/el/fire_research/R0000240.pdf)). The 0.7-versus-0.3 discrepancy is unresolved; it may reflect different definitions, boundaries or conditions. Neither value is the fraction evaporated, and the 0.3 here is not Särdqvist’s 0.3 upper limit.

A regulatory figure of **about 50% evaporated** appears in German Hessen guidance (2011), reported in a 2014 IChemE paper for fires of 200–600 m², said to be based on Karlsruhe research. The original Karlsruhe source was not obtained; this is an empirical/regulatory assumption for fire-water retention design, not a primary measurement ([IChemE Hazards XXIV](https://www.icheme.org/media/8931/xxiv-paper-37.pdf)). NISTIR 6191’s often-quoted **5–10%** is likewise a secondary estimate citing a training manual, with no primary dataset behind it ([NISTIR 6191, printed pp. 1-2–1-4](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir6191.pdf#page=10)).

| Heat-absorption efficiency | Blanchard et al. (2011) | 0.40–0.70, mean 0.53 | FDS reconstruction of a 1/3-scale tunnel | modelled, includes radiation/wall, fire not suppressed |

Within the sources located and inspected for this audit—NIST and IAFSS publications, agency libraries, publisher pages and general indexes, with no paywalls or bot challenges bypassed—**no measured fireground evaporation fraction or thermal efficiency was found**. That is a search result, not proof that none exists; closed studies such as Pretrel (2017) and the 2019 *Fire and Materials* paper could not be inspected.

## The engine can compare power only with a named η

The thermodynamic reference is sound and should be preserved exactly: liquid water at **20 °C and 101.325 kPa absolute** to vapor at **100 °C** at the same pressure has an enthalpy gain of **2591.5757 kJ/kg**, and one US gpm at the inlet density is 0.06297709 kg/s, giving **163.2099 kW per US gpm** ([NIST WebBook](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/fluid.cgi?Action=Data&Wide=on&ID=C7732185&Type=IsoBar&Digits=7&P=0.101325&THigh=100&TLow=20&TInc=80&RefState=DEF&TUnit=C&PUnit=MPa&DUnit=kg%2Fm3&HUnit=kJ%2Fkg&WUnit=m%2Fs&VisUnit=uPa*s&STUnit=N%2Fm)). This is a **full-vaporization reference capacity** for the declared states, not measured effectiveness and not an absolute maximum for hotter steam.

The minimum API discipline follows from the evidence. Expose one reference function, `referenceCoolingCapacityKw(deliveredAppliedFlowGpm)`, and one explicit comparison, `compareHeatReference({ deliveredAppliedFlowGpm, referenceHrrKw, etaHeat })`, that requires an `etaHeat` object carrying `value`, `basis` (`assumed` or `sourced`), and provenance. For an assumption, record the rationale; for a source, record the URL, locator, definition, applicability and uncertainty. **There must be no default η, including no silent value of 1**; a caller who wants only the ideal reference uses the first function. The comparison should require finite `Q ≥ 0`, finite `referenceHrrKw > 0`, and η in the declared [0,1] domain, reject invalid input, and return the reference capacity, adjusted capacity, signed power difference and ratio while preserving the supplied η provenance. It should not return `extinguished`, `safe`, a residual HRR, time to knockdown or any threshold verdict. Equality of adjusted capacity and fire HRR is only equality of two declared powers.

A sourced preset becomes defensible only when the source closes a **water-side heat balance** over a defined target and interval: measured applied mass flow, measured or otherwise closed steam/runoff accounting, inlet and outlet states, fire HRR, application method and geometry, and an uncertainty statement. A temperature-only measurement, a nozzle-label multiplier, a tactical flow factor or a secondary percentage is not enough. The evidence would also need to show applicability across the nozzle, fire size, compartment and application conditions the preset claims to represent; otherwise the value is a named scenario assumption, not a universal constant. Narrowly matched measurements may be offered with an explicit applicability label. This audit proposes no engine code, no presets and **no UI or scenario wiring**; screen work is out of scope.

## Conclusion

The corrected citation changes the meaning of the most-quoted number. **0.2 is not “20% of the water evaporates”**; it is 20% of a denominator already tripled for flame extinction, so it corresponds to 2.16 MJ/kg, or 60% of the 3.6 MJ/kg physical enthalpy to 600 °C steam. The apparent convergence between Särdqvist’s first test (`0.11 × 3 × 4.08 ≈ 1.35 MJ/kg`) and Barnett/Grimwood’s 50% of 2.6 MJ/kg (`≈ 1.30 MJ/kg`) is a **definitional coincidence across different denominators and assumptions**, not independent confirmation of a measured 50% or 11% efficiency.

The defensible engineering position is therefore deliberately narrow: keep the **163.21 kW/gpm** reference, force every adjusted result to display its η and its provenance, and decline to ship a default efficiency until a matched measurement exists. The audit’s main product is not a coefficient but a boundary: no transferable measured fireground efficiency was found, so the engine must not manufacture one.
